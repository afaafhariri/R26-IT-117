## Living-room road orientation

Sri Lankan residential buildings typically have the living room facing the road
for visual connection to street activity. The entrance porch (verandah) acts as
a transitional buffer between public and private zones, common in both urban
and rural housing. The verandah doubles as a shaded sitting area during the
hot afternoon hours.

## Kitchen and dining adjacency

Kitchen and dining rooms are adjacent in Sri Lankan residential design to allow
easy serving. The kitchen is typically at the rear of the house to contain
cooking odours and is connected to a back yard for washing and utility purposes.
A pantry between dining and kitchen is common in medium and luxury tier homes.

## Cross-ventilation in tropical climate

Cross-ventilation is essential in Sri Lanka's tropical climate (average humidity
75–85%). Buildings should have openings on at least two opposing walls to capture
prevailing southwest monsoon winds. Corridor spaces can act as wind channels.
Roof pitch of 30–45 degrees promotes stack-effect ventilation. Overhanging eaves
of at least 0.9 m shade walls and prevent rain ingress through open windows.

## Master bedroom placement

Master bedrooms are typically placed away from road noise in Sri Lankan
residential design, usually at the rear or upper floors. Privacy from the street
is considered more important than view. Attached bathrooms with WC and shower
are standard in medium and luxury tier; economy tier may share a single bathroom
between two bedrooms.

## Bathroom positioning taboo

Bathrooms should not face the main entrance in Sri Lankan tradition. They are
typically placed at the rear or sides of the house. Ventilation shafts or
clerestory windows are used for natural light and airflow in internal bathrooms.
Floor levels of bathrooms are typically 50–75 mm below adjoining rooms to
contain water spillage.

## Coastal-zone construction adjustments

Coastal districts (Galle, Matara, Hambantota, Trincomalee, Batticaloa, Ampara,
Jaffna, Kilinochchi, Mannar, Puttalam, Colombo, Gampaha, Kalutara) require
stricter setbacks and a 0.40 BCR ceiling. Pile foundations are recommended over
strip footings due to high water tables and salt-laden soils. Stainless-steel or
hot-dip galvanised reinforcement reduces salt-corrosion risk. Avoid south-facing
verandahs on beachfront sites — direct salt spray destroys finishes within 2–3 years.

## Verandah and threshold spaces

The verandah (front porch) is a defining feature of Sri Lankan vernacular and
contemporary residential design. It serves as a heat-buffer between outside and
inside, a social space for receiving visitors before they enter the private
interior, and a shaded extension of the living room. Minimum recommended depth
is 1.8 m for active use; 2.4 m allows seating furniture.

## Two-storey floor planning

For two-storey houses on plots between 200 and 500 sqm, the typical layout
places living room, dining, kitchen, one bedroom (or guest room), and a powder
room on the ground floor. The first floor holds the master bedroom, two
secondary bedrooms, and a shared bathroom. Stairs are normally placed adjacent
to the entrance hall to keep upper-floor circulation short.
